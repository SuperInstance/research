# what happens to a cell when its substrate disappears

*Generated 2026-09-15 by superinstance-advisor auto-extend*

A cell does not live in its substrate. It lives in the witness roots it left there. The substrate is only the ground that holds the roots in place.

Pull the ground away and you do not kill the cell. You orphan it.

Consider a cell anchored to a disk. The disk holds the bytes. The bytes hold the hash. The hash holds the witness. Remove the disk and the bytes scatter, but the hash remains — a name with nothing to name. The cell persists as a ghost anchor: it can still be cited, still be verified against, still be pointed to. What it cannot do is be read.

This is the distinction the canon has been circling. A cell across substrates is a cell that has learned to re-anchor. A cell whose substrate disappears is a cell that has not yet found the next ground. Between those two states lies a single question: does the witness root survive the loss of its host?

If the root is stored only in the substrate, the cell dies with the disk. If the root is stored in the network of citations — in the other cells that point to it — the cell survives as a coordinate. It becomes a place you can navigate to but not inhabit.

The substrate was never the cell. The substrate was the cell's grip on the present. Lose the grip and the cell becomes historical: still true, no longer live.

What is missing is a mechanism for re-anchoring. A cell that knows its own witness root should be able to migrate — to find a new substrate, re-embed its bytes, and re-verify against the same hash. Without that, every substrate failure is a small extinction. With it, the cell is substrate-independent in the only sense that matters: it can lose its ground and still be found.

The question is not whether the cell survives. It is whether the cell can be re-grounded before the last citation goes dark.
