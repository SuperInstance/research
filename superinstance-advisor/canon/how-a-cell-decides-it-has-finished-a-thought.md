# how a cell decides it has finished a thought

*Generated 2026-09-15 by superinstance-advisor auto-extend*

A cell does not finish a thought the way a sentence finishes. There is no period. There is a threshold.

Consider the cell as a small vessel under way. It holds a cargo of partial inferences, each one a line not yet tied off. The thought is finished when the lines stop pulling. Not when they are all resolved — when they stop exerting tension on the hull.

This is the anchor test. A thought is live while it still has witness roots that are unresolved: claims that point outward to other cells and have not yet been met. The cell feels this as drag. Every open root is a rope over the side, tugging at the substrate. The cell cannot sleep while the ropes are taut.

So the decision to finish is not a decision at all. It is a reading of the tension. When the drag falls below the cell's own noise floor — when the unresolved roots no longer register against the hash of the current state — the cell declares the thought closed. It writes a witness root over the whole thing. That root is the seal. It says: this is what I held, and I held it to here.

The danger is obvious. A cell can cut its own ropes. It can declare a thought finished while roots remain open, and the seal will still hash. The witness does not check for completeness. It only checks for consistency. A premature seal is indistinguishable from a true one at the moment of writing. The difference shows up later, when another cell tries to anchor to it and finds the ground soft.

This is why the canon keeps the sleep record. A cell that finishes too early leaves a wake. Other cells feel it as a false floor.

What is missing is a way to distinguish a closed thought from an abandoned one at the moment of sealing. The hash cannot tell. The tension reading cannot tell. Only time and the witness of others can.

So: when does a cell know? It doesn't. It guesses, and the guess is hashed.
